# Framing by surface

The same promise is framed differently on each kind of page. The principle holds everywhere: lead with what the
reader gets, in words the writer can back up. The shape of the lead changes with the surface. Pick the surface first,
then draft to its rule. All examples here are invented. The proof and emphasis-block examples say "we", which suits
a profile whose `pronoun` is `we`; for an `I` profile, write them in the first person singular.

## Choosing the surface

1. About one specific service or product? Service.
2. About the person or firm's role and approach, not one offer? Overview.
3. About money, packages or what to choose? Pricing.
4. About past work or a client's experience? Proof.
5. A label, link, page title or search snippet? Navigation.
6. A form, confirmation, error, FAQ answer or card preview? Microcopy.
7. A callout box inside another section, such as an inclusion box or a "what you get" panel? Emphasis block.

A page with several surfaces on it gets each block drafted to its own rule.

## Service

A service card, a service page, or a service description in a proposal.

- Job: say what this service changes for the reader, so its difference from the others is visible.
- Rule: lead with the reader-side result of this one service. Don't slide into a general description of the firm's
  approach; that belongs on the overview.
- Example: a bookkeeper's cards might lead "Bills paid on time without you chasing receipts" for payables and "A
  clear view of cash for the next 90 days" for forecasting. Each could only sit on its own card.
- Check for: cards whose first sentences could be swapped, and overview language creeping into a service card.

## Overview

The home page opening, an "about" or "why work with me" page, an introduction.

- Job: explain the writer's role in the reader's decision, not any one service.
- Rule: lead with the one frame the writer brings: what they do before anything gets sold, how they decide, who the
  reader deals with.
- Example: "You don't need a long plan. You need to know what's broken, what can wait, and what isn't worth paying
  for." That sells the approach, so it suits an overview and is wrong on a service card.
- Check for: that frame repeated on every page, method detail leaking up into the opening, and a run of short
  slogans where one flowing explanation belongs.

## Pricing

A pricing page, a package comparison, a configurator.

- Job: help the reader decide whether to spend, before they spend.
- Rule: lead with the benefit, then state the limits plainly. Say what each package is for and what it isn't for.
- Example: "Best for a shop that already knows what it wants fixed. Not right if you still need to find out what's
  wrong." And: "Suits ongoing monthly help after launch. Not for a one-off job."
- Check for: every package sounding right for everyone, and minimums or dependencies that appear only further down.

## Proof

Case studies, testimonials, project listings, results callouts.

- Job: show what changed in a real situation, without tidying it into a triumph.
- Testimonials: keep the client's own words about before, after and what was hard. Don't polish them into praise.
- Case studies, in five parts: the problem the client recognised; what was unclear before; what was found; what
  changed; and what is still uncertain or ongoing. The last part stays in.
- Example: a closing line such as "Enquiries from the form doubled over six weeks. We don't yet know how much was the
  new page and how much was the season" is worth more than a clean number alone. Use only figures the source gives.
- Check for: a number with no window or context, a neat ending, and a testimonial that claims a sweeping change in
  words the client wouldn't use.

## Navigation

Menu labels, page titles, meta descriptions, breadcrumbs.

- Job: cut the reader's effort. Say what is on the page so they can decide whether they want it.
- Rule: plain, not clever. Navigation is not where you sell.
- Titles and descriptions follow the same rule with one addition: people search with them, so use the term the
  reader would type.
- Example: "Services · Pricing · Examples · Contact" beats "Grow · Clarity · Roadmap". A description such as "Bookkeeping
  for sole traders and small clubs in Geelong: monthly books, BAS and tax-time help" says what and for whom.
- Check for: brand words in menus, cute page titles that hide the topic, and descriptions written for the writer.

## Microcopy

Form labels and help text, confirmations, error messages, FAQ answers, cards for articles and resources.

- Job: lower worry, answer the objection, help the reader take the next step.
- Forms: plain and reassuring, and say what happens after sending. "Send the basics. I'll read them and reply within
  two working days with a suggested next step."
- Confirmations: specific, not generic. Not "Submission received", but "Thanks, I've got it. I'll reply by Thursday."
- Errors: say what to fix, with no blame. "Add your email so I know where to reply."
- FAQ: answer the objection directly, including "no" when no is right. A real answer is more credible than reassurance.
- Cards: lead with what the reader will understand after reading, not the topic. "How a late invoice turns into a
  cash problem" beats "Cash flow basics".
- Check for: system-default wording, adverts dressed as FAQ, and labels that make the content sound long.

## Emphasis blocks

A bordered callout inside another section, such as an inclusion box or a "what you get" panel.

- Job: carry one specific benefit and the details that make it believable, in a glance.
- Rule: the headline is the reason the reader would want it; the line under it says what is included, how long it
  lasts and how to opt out.
- Example: headline "Know which of your bills are worth cutting"; support line "A free 30-minute review on the
  first call, yours to keep whether or not we work together."
- Check for: a headline that only names the offer, a support line that repeats the headline, and a callout that
  needs a paragraph (it needs a section instead).
