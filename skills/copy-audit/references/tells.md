# Copy tells
<!-- plainspoken: quotes tells on purpose -->

These patterns pass a word check and still read as written by a template. Each entry says what it looks like, why it
fails the reader and what to do. Examples are invented. In every fix, each fact comes from the original line; where
the original gives nothing to say, the fix leaves a bracketed gap for the writer. A tell seen once is a hypothesis
about this piece of copy. Word-level tells (banned words, buzzwords) come from the checker, not from this list.

### a. Adjective triple in headings, and the H1 that doesn't say what happens

Three stacked adjectives in a heading read as marketing rhythm. The H1 is the line with the most reader attention, so
a vague one costs the most.

Why it fails: the adjectives describe the writer's opinion of the work, and the reader learns nothing they can use.

Example: "Fast, friendly, flexible bookkeeping" for a sole bookkeeper. Fix: say the job of the adjective, for example
"Bookkeeping done by one person [Writer: say what the client can count on, such as turnaround or how they reach
you]". Keep one plain adjective at most, and only with a concrete promise beside it. Then test the H1 on its own:
cover the rest of the page and ask whether the reader could say what happens if they read on.

### b. Abstract-noun stacking

Two or more abstract nouns chained with soft verbs: "We help teams build clearer plans, stronger habits and lasting
results."

Why it fails: nothing can be pictured, so nothing is remembered. It sounds like benefit and names none.

Fix: cut to one noun and ground it in what the reader will have: "You'll have a plan [Writer: say what the plan
lets the reader decide or do this month]". A result a person can hold beats three they can't.

### c. Performed thoroughness in the opening (and its sibling, metaphor reaching for warmth)

The first lines pile up credentials ("research-led, experience-backed, built on years of practice") or comfort ("I
see you, I get it, you're doing great") before saying what changes for the reader. The sibling reaches for a homely
image, such as a kettle on and the dog asleep, that does no work the plain words couldn't.

Why it fails: the reader came with a question and gets a performance. Warmth and expertise land when they're shown in
the specifics, not announced.

Fix: find the first sentence that says what changes for the reader, and cut everything above it. Keep a metaphor only
when the literal sentence can't do its job.

### e. In-house vocabulary leaking to readers

Internal product names, plan names, taxonomy codes or process labels used as nouns the reader has never met: "Your
Tier 2 plan includes a Foundation Sprint."

Why it fails: it reads like the filing system. The reader has to learn your vocabulary before they can read your offer.

Fix: say what it is in the reader's words with the benefit attached: "[Writer: what the Foundation Sprint gives the
reader, and how long it takes]". If the name has to appear, explain it the first time.

### f. A list of methods as the hero

The top of the page is an inventory of problems or techniques: "Slow pages. Broken forms. Missing tags. Weak
headlines."

Why it fails: it's a diagnosis with no offer. The reader sees what is wrong and not what will change.

Fix: if the hero names problems, it must also name the change. Keep the list lower down as evidence.

### g. A method sold as the service

A multi-step process becomes the differentiator: "My four-stage framework", "a six-week programme", with what the
reader ends up with unclear.

Why it fails: the process is how the work happens, and the reader is buying the outcome. A page that headlines the
process makes the reader reconstruct the outcome from it.

Example: "My four-stage process: Listen, Map, Build, Review" as the headline of a bookkeeping page. Fix: lead with
what the reader has at the end, "[Writer: what the client has once the work is done, such as books up to date and
what they can do with them]". Move the four stages below, where a reader who wants the detail will find them.

### h. Pronoun drift against the profile's stance

The profile says "I" (a solo writer) and the page slips into "we", "our team", "our approach", or the reverse for a
real team.

Why it fails: the reader notices a person turning into a company halfway down the page and trusts both less.

Example: with a solo "I" profile, a page that opens "I build websites for local clubs" and later says "Our team will
review your brief". Fix: "I'll review your brief", with the stance held to the end. If there is a team, say who is
in it, for example "[Writer: who reviews the brief]".

### i. Defensive reassurance

Pre-emptive comfort about a worry the reader didn't raise: "No jargon, no lock-in, no nasty surprises."

Why it fails: it answers the writer's grievance with other providers, not the reader's question, and it plants the
worry it claims to remove.

Fix: say what the reader gets, positively: "[Writer: what working with you is like, such as who does the work and
how contract terms read]". Keep a negative only when it is true, relevant and the reader's own concern.

### j. Buzzword drift inside otherwise plain copy

A single stock word survives in a warm, plain sentence because the sentence around it feels fine.

Why it fails: one stock word makes the line sound like every other provider's page.

Fix: run the checker. It finds these by word, and this file deliberately doesn't repeat its lists.

### k. Numbered claims with nothing behind them

A heading promises a count ("Three clear answers", "Five reasons") and the body never lists them.

Why it fails: the reader is primed to count and can't. The number was a credibility move the body didn't earn.

Example: a heading "Five reasons to choose a local bike shop" over two paragraphs of general talk. Fix: list the
five in that block as a numbered list, each with the writer's own reason ("[Writer: reason 1 to 5]"), or change the
heading to a plain statement: "[Writer: the one reason this shop is worth choosing]".

### l. A constraint doing the job of the lead

On an overview or service card, the first line is a minimum spend, a platform limit or an exclusion: "From $900 a
month, one platform only. Campaigns built around what people actually search for."

Why it fails: the reader sees the cost before the benefit and leaves. The limit is true, so hiding it would be worse.

Fix: lead with the benefit and give the limit a clearly visible practical-notes line beneath the list. Disclosure
stays; only its position changes.

### m. Defining yourself by what you aren't

A lead built on contrast with other providers ("Not an agency. No account managers."). The plain-language skill covers it in full, under "Defining by what it isn't"; use that guidance.

### n. A verdict on the reader's current setup

A sentence that judges what the reader already has: "Your website is outdated and your ads are wasting money."

Why it fails: the reader hasn't asked for a verdict, you haven't seen their numbers, and it puts them on the
defensive.

Fix: name the after-state instead: "[Writer: what the reader's site and ads will do once you've worked on them]". A
brief, honest recognition ("if enquiries have gone quiet") works sparingly as a doorway, followed straight away by
what changes.

When the reader asked for the assessment (a review, an audit, a reply to "what's wrong?"), the verdict is the job: state
the problem plainly, as a fact about the work and not the person, with its evidence beside it.

### o. Stop-start rhythm

Body copy made of separate slogans ("No fuss. No jargon. Just results."). The plain-language skill covers it in full, in its rhythm guidance; here, flag three or more unconnected adjacent lines and send the fix there.

### p. Posture that doesn't fit the profile's locale

The words pass, but the stance is another market's: heroic and motivational ("Unlock growth with an end-to-end
roadmap built to transform your presence") where the profile's locale wants a capable person explaining the work. The
opposite failure is reaching for local slang to sound at home.

Why it fails: readers hear the borrowed posture before they read the words.

Fix: write as a person would explain it aloud to the reader: "I'll look at your presence [Writer: name the real
things you'll look at, and what the reader gets]." Take spelling and idiom from the profile's locale, and don't add
slang as seasoning.
