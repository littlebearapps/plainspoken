# Messaging principle

## The principle

Lead with what the reader gets, in words the writer can stand behind.

That is two halves, and copy fails if either one is missing. Put the reader's experience first and the method
second, and describe that experience only as strongly as the writer can back it up. Get the first half wrong and the
copy reads like a technical menu. Get the second wrong and it reads like a sales deck.

Persuasive copy and plain copy are the same thing here. A plain, specific line that a capable person could say aloud
is usually sharper than a polished one.

Lead with the gain, not the absence of a pain. A brief nod to the reader's situation can open the door, but the weight
of the copy sits on what they will have afterwards.

## Two layers

Every page, section and card has two layers.

- **Top layer.** What changes for the reader, in their own words. Most of the space goes here, and it is the part
  they could repeat to a friend.
- **Bottom layer.** Method, tools, process, minimums and limits, for the reader who wants detail before they trust
  the claim. It builds trust and it stays visible.

The proportions flex from one surface to the next. The order doesn't: benefit first, method second. The exceptions
are surfaces whose job is practical (navigation, legal text, a price table, an error message), which can lead with
the fact the reader needs.

## The three tests

Every top-layer line should pass all three.

1. **Friend test.** Could the reader repeat this to a friend over coffee? "A bike shop that fixes your commuter bike
   the same day" passes. "Full drivetrain diagnostics with torque-spec reassembly" doesn't, so it moves to the bottom
   layer or off the page.
2. **Control test.** Can the writer do this directly, or only influence it? "Built to make booking easier" is
   something the writer controls. "Will fill your diary" depends on the reader's market and on other people, so the
   verb gets softened to the part the writer does.
3. **Capacity test.** Does the line recognise the reader's real constraint? Most readers don't want to become experts
   in the writer's field, and many fear paying for something they'll then have to manage. A line that is plain and
   honest can still feel like one more job. "You keep running the bakery; I handle the ordering system" removes that
   worry. "Powerful tools to manage your orders" adds to it.

## Truth and control

Promise the levers the writer controls: what is built, written, set up, configured or delivered. Report the signals
the writer can observe, such as what is being measured and what changed. Never promise the outcome that depends on
other people, on a market or on a platform's decisions. More evidence doesn't bring an outcome under the writer's
control, so don't word the limit as if it's only waiting on data ("not until I've seen the numbers"): the numbers
can show which lever to pull first, but the outcome still isn't the writer's to promise.

In practice, that means the strong, direct verb goes with the writer's own work ("I'll set up", "I'll write",
"designed to") and a softer one goes with anything that depends on someone else ("makes it easier for people to
find", "gives you a better chance of"). A concrete number is fine when it describes the writer's work, such as hours
spent or pages delivered, and not when it predicts the reader's result. Softening should leave a sentence that is
still specific, because a vague line is as much a failure as an overclaim.
