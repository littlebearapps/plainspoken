# Section design

Use this when the task includes page or section layout. A section has to hang together before it can differ from the
section next to it. Every example is invented.

## Section shapes vary

Name each section's shape while sketching the page: a prose paragraph, a card grid, a decision helper, a checklist, a
quote or boxout, an expandable list, a plain bulleted list. No three consecutive sections should share a shape.
Repeating one shape makes a page read as a form that was filled in, and it is much cheaper to catch at sketch time
than after drafting.

A plain-shaped example: a bike shop page that runs a prose intro, then a boxout for the same-day promise, then a
price table, then a decision helper ("which service do I need?"), then a short list of what to bring. No shape
repeats three times, so the rhythm holds.

## Items in a row vary on one axis

When a section uses parallel structure (bullets, cards, columns), ask what the one thing is that the items vary on.
If they mix criteria, the reader scans for a pattern and can't find one, and it reads as a grab-bag.

Example: a community radio page lists "good fit" as four bullets: not-for-profits (type of organisation), a venue
that already has a stage (current state), a preference for plain advice (attitude) and a budget under $500 a month
(money). That is four axes in one list. Either key every item on one axis (three cards for three kinds of
organisation, with the other criteria in each card's text), or put the mixed criteria into one profile sentence,
where prose can hold several dimensions at once.

## Positives lead, exclusions sit beneath

A fit-check section exists to help the right reader recognise themselves. Honest exclusions are the safety net, and
they shouldn't be given the same weight as the positive picture. Two equal columns, "Best fit" and "Not a fit", look
like a balance sheet, because the eye reads visual weight before it reads words.

Check by covering the words and asking what the layout says. If it says "equal priority", demote the exclusions to a
short note beneath, such as "Not for you if you want a same-week turnaround." The content is the same and the weight
is honest.

## The heading fits the section

Whenever a section's shape changes, re-read its heading against the new content. A title left over from the old
shape promises something the section no longer does, and the reader meets the heading before the body.

Example: after the "Not a fit" column becomes a footnote, the heading "Honest about who I'm for" still implies a
balanced disclosure. "Who I work best with" promises exactly what the section now delivers.

## Don't build sections that force taglines

In explanatory sections, don't chop every idea into cards, fragments or single-line claims for the sake of
scanning. Cards and short lines suit comparison, navigation and emphasis. Prose suits the places where the reader
needs context, reassurance or the logic of what happens next. A section built entirely from stacked one-liners puts
the copy into stop-start rhythm before anyone has written a word.

The three within-section checks above (one axis, positives lead, a heading that fits) fail together more often than
alone. When one changes, re-check the other two, because a new row of items often needs a new weight and a new title.

## One main action per page

Every page resolves to one named action. If a second one exists, it is a text link beneath the main button, not a
parallel button. A reader offered two equal choices tends to do neither.

Example: a bakery page with "Order a cake" and "Send us a message" as twin buttons. Make "Order a cake" the button
and put "Prefer to write? Send a message instead" beneath it as a link. The exception is a page whose job is to help
the reader choose between packages, which may carry two.

When a second offer exists, such as a free sample or a checklist, absorb it into the first: "Every order includes a
free tasting box" keeps one path. "Two ways forward" splits it.

## Say what happens next

Every call to action tells the reader what happens when they use it. "Learn more" and "Contact us" leave them
guessing what's on the other side. "Book a 20-minute fitting" or "Order a cake for 10 days from now" names the thing
about to happen, and often the time or cost, so the reader can picture the next step before committing to it.
