# Worked examples

Every business below is invented, and so are its numbers and its people. They show a reasoning move, not wording to
copy. A rewrite in the next piece of copy will have a different reader and different facts, so use the reasoning.

In each example the after line adds nothing the before line didn't already say. Where the original gave no benefit to
state, the after line leaves a bracketed gap for the writer. Never fill the gap with a guess.

### Example 1: a bike shop, method leading the lead

**Before:** "Full drivetrain service, hydraulic brake bleeds and wheel truing by qualified mechanics, using
manufacturer torque specifications."

**After:** "[Writer: what the rider has once the bike comes back, and how soon.] Drivetrain, brakes and wheels are all
done by qualified mechanics to the manufacturer's torque specifications."

**Reasoning.** The before line fails the friend test: nobody repeats "hydraulic brake bleeds" over coffee. It lists
activities, so it fails the outcomes gate. The move is to ask what the rider has at the end (a bike that works), say
that first, and keep the method as one trust-building line beneath it. Nothing was added to the facts. Drivetrain,
brakes, wheels, the mechanics and the torque specifications were all in the original. The benefit the original never
stated is a bracketed gap, and the rider's end state is for the writer to fill.

### Example 2: a community radio station, a promise it doesn't control

**Before:** "Sponsor us and we'll bring hundreds of new customers through your door."

**After:** "Sponsor us. [Writer: what the sponsor actually gets, and the audience figure with where it comes from.]"

**Reasoning.** This one fails the control test. The station controls what it broadcasts. It doesn't control whether a
listener walks into a shop, and "hundreds" is a number nobody can promise. The after line drops the sales outcome
and holds a place for what the sponsor actually gets, with a real, sourced audience figure. The test is whether a
sceptical sponsor could check the claim: they can check what goes to air, and they can't check a sales outcome.

### Example 3: a family bakery, a constraint doing the job of the lead

**Before:** "Custom cakes need a minimum of 10 days' notice and a $60 deposit. Our cakes are made fresh to order."

**After:** "Our cakes are made fresh to order. [Writer: what the customer ends up with, and how to start an order.]
Custom cakes need a minimum of 10 days' notice and a $60 deposit."

**Reasoning.** The notice period and deposit are true and relevant, so hiding them would be a mistake, and the
checklist's constraint-honesty item would fail. But as the first line they make the reader meet a rule before a
benefit. The move is to keep the constraint, still visible, and let the benefit open. The fix reorders what the
original said and adds no fact. "Made fresh to order" is the only benefit the original gave, so it leads, and the
rest of the benefit is a gap for the writer.

### Example 4: a one-person bookkeeping practice, pronoun drift and defensive reassurance

**Before:** "We're not a big firm. No jargon, no hidden fees, no lock-in contracts. Our team will take care of your
books."

**After:** "I take care of your books. [Writer: what that covers and when the client sees it, and how the contract
terms read.]"

**Reasoning.** Two things move at once. The profile is a solo writer, so "we" and "our team" is pronoun drift: a
person becomes a company halfway down the page. And the first two sentences are defensive reassurance: they answer
complaints about other providers before the reader has raised one, and they define the practice by what it isn't.
The fix takes the stance from the profile ("I"), keeps the one thing the original actually does (takes care of the
books), and leaves a gap for the specifics it never gave. The negatives are dropped rather than rephrased, because
the original didn't show they were true and relevant to this reader.
