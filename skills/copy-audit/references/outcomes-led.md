# Outcomes-led copy

## Outcomes lead, method follows

Say what changes for the reader before saying how it happens. The reader has a problem or a goal, and the first lines
they see should show that you understand it and name the difference you make. How it works comes next, for the reader
who wants to check before trusting.

A useful drafting order for a block of copy:

1. The reader's after-state, in their words: what they have, can do or no longer have to worry about.
2. One line on how, in plain terms, including who does the work.
3. Anything that limits it (a minimum, a dependency, a lead time), placed where the reader will see it.
4. A single next step.

Method isn't a bad word. It earns its place when it answers "how do I know this is real?", and it belongs lower down
than the benefit. A tool name, a step count or a technique leading the page means the writer's work has been put
ahead of the reader's gain.

Test each service or offer by asking what a reader has at the end that they didn't have at the start. If the answer
is a list of activities, the offer is still described as a method. Rewrite from the end state backwards.

Where two offers share a shape (the same kind of channel, similar prices), the lead line must carry the one
difference that explains why someone would pick one over the other. Without it the two read as interchangeable.

## Framing by surface

Each surface (page, email, post, ad, proof, microcopy) frames the same promise differently. The adapt skill holds the
full guidance, so check it there rather than here.

## What to watch for

The common failures are method leaking into the lead, overclaiming verbs, soft abstractions and benefit-shaped
vagueness. The plain-language skill holds the full patterns, so use it for the detail.
