# Rhythm

Explanatory text should sound like one person explaining. This file covers the one rhythm failure that costs readers
the most, and the two ways of overcorrecting for it.

## Stop-start rhythm

Every sentence restarts from zero: a claim, a fragment, a contrast, another claim. No thought leads into the next, so
the text reads as a chant rather than as someone explaining their work. The tell is repetition that hasn't been
earned. Length isn't the tell.

Common shapes:

- a run of parallel clauses with the same subject and verb, each listing an adjacent idea as if it were unrelated;
- a series of "no X, no Y, just Z" or "not this, not that" lines;
- a row of slogans with nothing joining them.

Short lines earn their place in headlines, buttons, section labels, card titles, a transition, and one line of
emphasis after the explanation has landed. They fail in body copy, answers and opening paragraphs, where the reader
needs the reason or the next step.

The fix is to join ideas that belong together, with a plain connective where the relationship is real: and, but, so,
because, if, once, while, instead, which means, that way. Read it aloud. If each sentence feels like a fresh start
instead of a continuation, bind the ones that belong together.

Before:

> I check your site. I read your ads. I list the gaps. Then I fix the worst ones.

After:

> I'll check your site and read your ads, list the gaps, then fix the worst ones.

Three disconnected claims in a row is a reason to look, not an automatic fault. Ask whether the lines explain a
sequence, a cause or a real contrast. If they're stacked only for punch, rewrite.

## Over-correction guardrails

- **Don't stretch short sentences to hit a number.** Padding a plain idea into a long sentence fails the reader a
  different way.
- **Don't join unrelated ideas.** A comma or "and" between two things that have nothing to do with each other is
  worse than two sentences.
- **Keep short lines for headlines, buttons and emphasis.** Short, sharp language is part of most writers' voices.
- **Mixed lengths are the goal.** There is no length cap and no minimum. Some short lines and some connected
  explanation sounds like a person thinking.

## How it's checked

The read-aloud test: read the passage out as the reader. If each sentence feels like a fresh start, join the ones
that belong together; if a sentence runs out of breath, split it. By default, on an article, the checker flags the
extremes with a warning: a run of five or more sentences under 12 words, a section with no sentence over 25 words,
and five or more sentences of near-equal length. These are the default thresholds: social posts use tighter ones, and
a profile's `[thresholds]` can change any of them. A flag is a prompt to read it aloud, not an instruction to hit a
number.
