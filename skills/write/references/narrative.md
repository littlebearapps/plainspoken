# Narrative

A story earns its point instead of stating it up front. The reader stays because something happens.

## Open on a scene or a moment

Start in a place, at a time, with something happening. Five openings that work:

- **Arrival.** The writer gets somewhere. Weather, sound, light, smell: the way in is half the story.
  "The roller door was already up when I got there, and the radio in the back was playing to nobody."
- **A small truth, then the case.** One general line, then a turn to the specific example.
  "Some jobs you finish and forget. The fence on Quellbrook Lane wasn't one of them."
- **One person.** Someone named by a habit or an oddity, described in physical detail.
- **One number.** A single figure that carries the whole piece, given in the first line.
  "I've rewritten the same quote 14 times."
- **The odd one out.** Something that made the writer unusual in a setting, used as the lens for the rest.

Pick the one that suits what the sources hold. An opening needs a real moment from the sources or the user; never
invent one.

## An arc

Something changes between the first section and the last: a decision made, a view revised, a problem understood.
Plan the turn before drafting. A piece that ends where it started is a list of scenes.

## Detail from the sources

Sensory detail (what the place looked like, sounded like, felt like) comes from the sources or the user. Ask for it
if the piece needs it and the sources don't have it. Names, dates and places are claims, like any other.

## Uneven rhythm

Let paragraphs vary: a one-sentence paragraph next to a long one, a short sentence after a run-on one. Let one aside
wander off and come back. Rhythm follows the story, not a pattern.

## End on something new

The last paragraph adds something: an image, a consequence, an unanswered question, a thing the writer still
doesn't know. It doesn't recap what the reader just read.

## Markers to check after drafting

- The opening is a scene or a moment, not a definition or a summary.
- At least one specific date, figure or place, with its source.
- The writer's opinion appears at least once, in the way the profile's `opinion pattern:` line describes.
- At least one sensory detail, drawn from the sources.
- Paragraph lengths vary: at least one one-sentence paragraph and one long one.
- The ending adds something rather than summing up.
