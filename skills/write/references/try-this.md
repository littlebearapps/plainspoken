# Things the reader can try

For a piece whose reader-value contract asks the reader to do something (`type` `task`, `decision`, `self_check`,
`implementation` or `debugging_path`), put the steps where the reader meets the idea, then gather them at the end.
Skip this for history, commentary and essays: never force steps onto a piece that doesn't need them.

Every tool, link and prompt comes from the sources, the brief or the user, like any other fact. A tool you remember
but haven't checked this session doesn't go in.

## Inline: one step where it belongs

A blockquote that starts `**Try this:**`, straight after the idea it tests:

> **Try this:** Search for your cafe's name on [Example Maps](https://example.com/maps) from a phone. If the opening
> hours are wrong there, fix them before you touch the website.

- One step per callout, with its link written as a markdown link.
- One sentence after the link on why it matters or what to look for. Two or three sentences in all; a longer
  walkthrough belongs in the body.
- Free tools first. Mark a paid one `(paid)`, and use it only when there's no free one.
- Name a tool once. Later, point back to it ("the maps check above") rather than repeating the callout.
- For a prompt the reader can paste, start with `**Try this prompt:**`, then the prompt in quotes, then what a weak
  answer tells them.

## At the end: one list of everything

A `## Tools and resources` section before the FAQ (the FAQ stays last), opening with one line on what it holds. Use
only the subheadings you have items for: `### Free tools`, `### Sample prompts`, `### Checklists and templates`,
`### Further reading`. Each item is a link and what it's for, in one line.

## What counts

The test is whether a step works for the reader without more research, not how many callouts there are. The
reader-value deliver mode checks that against the contract; two callouts that work beat five that don't.
