# Australian English guide

For people writing in Australian English. The drafting skills don't load this guide. The checker's Australian word
data lives in `data/locales/en-AU.toml`, and it changes only through a kaizen proposal (see
`docs/kaizen-candidates.md`, K2), never by editing a word list in a prompt.

Localising means writing the way a capable local would, not swapping in slang. Most of it is spelling, wording,
rhythm and posture. Copy that reads as American with Australian spelling sprinkled on top is the thing to avoid.

## Rule levels

Not every flagged word is wrong. Four levels decide what to do with a hit.

| Level | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Replace | Almost always wrong in Australian copy | Swap it. Exceptions: a quotation, a brand or product name, code |
| Prefer | The Australian or plainer term is the default | Swap it, and note any reason you didn't |
| Challenge | Not banned, but ask if a local reader would say it | Read it aloud and decide in context |
| Keep | A name or term deliberately fixed | Leave it exactly as written |

When in doubt, choose the plainer version.

## Proper names keep their spelling

A product, company, service or programme name keeps its own spelling, whatever the rest of the page does. A name with
a US spelling stays as it is. The same goes for quotations, titles of works, code identifiers and file names. Don't
"correct" these, and don't let a search-and-replace do it.

## Posture

Australian writing usually sounds clear, practical, calm and a little sceptical of hype. It is willing to say what
isn't worth doing. It doesn't sound motivational, over-packaged or breathless.

Two failures to watch together:

1. Over-polished promotional writing in an American register.
2. Over-Australianising: reaching for slang to sound local. Plain beats affected.

Posture is about voice, not vocabulary. A paragraph can pass every word swap and still fail by sounding slick.

## Enquiry or inquiry

Use enquiry for ordinary questions and requests for information from customers, members or the public. Use inquiry
for formal investigations and official processes (a parliamentary inquiry), or where it's a fixed name.

Example: "Their site looks fine, but it isn't bringing in enough enquiries."

## Money, dates and times

- Money: `$49` when Australian dollars are obvious; `A$49` when another dollar could be confused; `US$49` for US
  dollars. Say "inc GST" or "ex GST" when it matters. Avoid `AUD $49` and `$49 AUD`.
- Dates: day, month, year, as in `22 July 2026`. `22/7/2026` only where space is tight.
- Time: `9 am`, `5:30 pm`, midday, midnight.
- Measurements: metric (mm, cm, km, kg, mL, L, °C).

## Imported idioms

Don't use a phrase just because it's common in American or generic internet English. Ask whether a local would say
something close to it. The principle is to prefer the word a local would use, for example "footpath" for "sidewalk"
and "car park" for "parking lot". A local reader will also say "mobile" where an American says "cell phone".
The checker's data holds the rest of the list.

## Informal Australian terms

Use these sparingly and only where they fit the voice. Some are fine in casual business writing. A few are fine
one at a time and never stacked. Broad stereotypes are usually wrong for professional writing unless the voice
calls for them. The checker's data holds the specific terms.

## The naturalness test

Before you finish a block of text, ask:

1. Would a normal local reader understand it straight away?
2. Would someone say something close to this out loud?
3. Does it sound like a person explaining, or like software or agency positioning?
4. Is the phrasing imported from American marketing or generic web copy?
5. Could it be said more plainly without losing meaning?
6. Is it trying too hard to sound Australian?
7. Would it sound credible read aloud on a call?

When in doubt, choose the plainer version.
